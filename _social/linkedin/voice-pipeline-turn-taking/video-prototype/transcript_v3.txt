 1. Talking to an AI can be genuinely powerful.
 2. Brainstorming out loud, on a walk.
 3. Speaking instructions instead of typing them, while you're vibe-coding.
 4. Or steering a robot — where there's no keyboard to type on in the first place.
 5. And speech-to-speech can be build based on three simple components: speech-to-text, an LLM as the agent in the middle, text-to-speech. Three models, stitched together.
 6. This already exists as a streaming API you can just call — for example Google's Streaming API, OpenAI's Realtime API.
 7. But there's a catch: you're sending raw audio back and forth, and you don't have full control over the agent sitting in the middle.
 8. The alternative: run speech-to-text and text-to-speech locally. Only text ever leaves the device.
 9. But now, if you want it to actually feel good to use, a lot of that logic is on you. Luckily, Huggingface already did the work and open-sourced a reference build — there's a lot to learn about what a good user experience actually takes, just from looking closely at it.
10. We need one more component for a smooth pipeline: voice activity detection, or VAD. It's a lightweight classifier that listens to the audio and flags when a stretch of silence looks like a pause.
11. And that brings us to the first problem: latency. Wait for one big block of audio, and transcription only starts once you're already done talking. Huggingface solves this part without even needing VAD.
12. So instead: chunk it, and keep re-transcribing in the background while you're still speaking.
13. Audio comes in as a stream, cut into fixed-size chunks.
14. A second chunk arrives, still no transcript yet.
15. Two chunks in, and the first transcript catches up — transcribing them took about as long as this third chunk took to arrive.
16. A fourth chunk comes in, same story.
17. The second transcript catches up too. The fifth chunk is still just audio — nobody's transcribed it yet, because you're still speaking.
18. Now that speech-to-text is fast, we also need the LLM's response to be fast. That's where Huggingface uses a smart trick — and this is where VAD actually comes in.
19. Here's what that looks like under the hood. You start speaking, and speech-to-text starts re-transcribing everything every half second — not waiting for a pause, just running on a fixed clock.
20. You pause. Just thirty-two milliseconds of silence — far shorter than a real pause needs to be.
21. But that's already enough to fire a speculative reply, based on whatever's been said so far.
22. You keep talking. The grace window was still open, so that's allowed.
23. The speculative reply gets thrown away — but the transcript never stopped. Only the guess was wasted.
24. A real pause. Thirty-two milliseconds fires again.
25. A new speculative reply fires immediately, this time based on the full sentence.
26. Nothing interrupts it. A full second of silence passes, uninterrupted.
27. The gate opens. The reply was already sitting there, ready.
28. Speech starts almost immediately — because the LLM's answer was already there, waiting, and now it streams straight into text-to-speech too.
29. And the very last piece: don't wait for the LLM's full reply either. Stream tokens straight into text-to-speech as they're generated.
30. That's the Huggingface real-time pipeline — and for this use case, it's genuinely great UX: a fast, low-latency conversation. But it's a bad fit for long pauses, for actually thinking out loud — and that's often exactly my use case.
31. So here's the alternative I actually use instead: I manually decide when to hand the turn over — and I run my own, personal agent in the middle, rather than a general-purpose one.
32. Here's how that actually works. You start speaking.
33. You pause. VAD notices — a chunk boundary gets marked and the transcription starts with the existing audio.
34. You keep talking, while that first chunk finishes transcribing in the background — speech-to-text always lags a little behind.
35. You decide you're done, and press send — no VAD needed here. The manual trigger fires on its own, whether or not a pause was ever detected. Which means you can record audio with pauses built right into it — exactly what happens to me when I'm still sorting out my thoughts, using an AI agent as a sparring partner.
36. One more transcription to catch up on — the last stretch of audio still has to go through speech-to-text before anything can be sent.
37. Both chunks arrive at the LLM together.
38. The reply starts streaming. First token out — but nothing's spoken yet, text-to-speech needs a little buffer before it can start.
39. Speech catches up, one token behind.
40. Streaming continues, still one step behind.
41. And the last word is spoken. No more tokens coming — text-to-speech just finishes what it already has.
42. For the best experience, you need a bit more logic still — take wake word detection, for example.
43. It's a small, always-on model listening for nothing but a single trigger phrase — "Hey Google," "Alexa" — cheap enough to run continuously, without waking the rest of the pipeline.
44. Then there's barge-in: interrupting the reply before it's finished. You need a signal for that.
45. The easy way is a push button — press it, and the assistant stops talking.
46. Otherwise, the signal is just you speaking again. But that means detecting your voice while the speaker is still playing the reply back — harder than it sounds.
47. On headphones, that's not a problem. Without them, you need real acoustic echo cancellation hardware.
48. Or you sidestep the echo problem entirely: use a wake word again, so the assistant can tell your voice apart from its own.
